Battery Energy Storage System for 20 Indian Industries That Need to Quit Diesel

November 21, 20268 min read

A Battery Energy Storage System (BESS) stores electricity in rechargeable batteries and discharges it on demand, in milliseconds, to run equipment or back up a site during an outage. For Indian industries still relying on diesel generators, BESS runs at a lower cost per unit, switches in instantly instead of in 30 seconds, produces zero on-site emissions, and is now the component India’s power policy is actively steering new industrial and renewable capacity toward. The rest of this guide covers how BESS works, why diesel still dominates industrial backup power in India, a direct comparison between the two technologies, where BESS adoption pays off fastest, and how to choose the right system from the UnityESS product range for your site.

What is a Battery Energy Storage System (BESS)?

A battery energy storage system for industries is a modular bank of batteries, typically lithium iron phosphate (LFP) cells, paired with an inverter and a battery management system (BMS) that controls charging and discharging. It charges from the grid, from on-site solar, or both, and discharges that stored power back into a facility's load, either instantly during a grid outage or on a schedule to shift consumption away from expensive peak-tariff hours.

For industrial and commercial (C&I) sites, a BESS does more than back up the grid. It is used for peak shaving (discharging during high-tariff windows to cut demand charges), open-access optimisation (storing power bought at lower rates for use later), renewable integration (storing solar generated during the day for use at night), and power quality correction (riding through voltage dips and brownouts that would otherwise damage sensitive equipment). This is the shift the Indian Energy Storage Alliance (IESA) and Customized Energy Solutions describe as a move "from backup-centric to application-driven energy storage solution". Why diesel generators still dominate industrial backup power in India

India's diesel generator market is valued at roughly USD 1.33 billion in 2026 and is projected to cross USD 1.88 billion by 2031, growing faster than the global average. That is not a market in decline. It reflects how many Indian industries still pay for power twice: once to the grid, once to diesel, because grid supply alone cannot be trusted to stay on.

CSTEP's 2025 study Switch on, Smoke off counted 14.7 lakh diesel generator (DG) sets above 5 kVA operating in India, with a combined capacity of over 2.7 lakh MVA, and named them a major hyperlocal source of PM2.5 pollution across cities including Bengaluru, Mumbai and Patna. The telecom sector alone burns through an estimated 2.5 to 3.2 billion litres of diesel a year keeping towers on during outages, an application where compact cabinet systems like UnityESS Aura can eliminate diesel dependence entirely.

Diesel generators run at roughly ₹30-35 per unit on a fuel-led cost basis, with 38-42% efficiency and a 30 second response time, and that cost is rising: diesel is subsidised and imported, which exposes industrial users to currency and fiscal-policy swings on top of global oil prices. A mid-size hospital in Delhi NCR, for comparison, reports monthly diesel bills of ₹6-8 lakh just to keep critical equipment running through outages.

Benefits of adopting BESS for industries in India

  • Lower cost of backup power. No fuel purchase, transport or storage, and no exposure to diesel price volatility or subsidy changes.
  • Instant, reliable switchover. A sub-20ms transition protects sensitive equipment, from ventilators to PLC-controlled machinery, that a diesel generator's 30 second start-up cannot.
  • Lower regulatory and compliance risk. No CPCB emission-norm filings, acoustic enclosures or NGT exposure tied to DG set pollution.
  • Revenue and savings beyond backup. Peak shaving and open-access optimisation let a BESS cut demand charges and electricity bills even on days with no outage, unlike a diesel generator that only costs money when it runs.
  • Built for India's policy direction. The Ministry of Power now mandates storage in new solar capacity, and the Central Electricity Authority has built standalone BESS into its resource-adequacy planning through 2035-36. Read more in our analysis on why BESS is no longer optional for India.
  • Scales with the business. Modular systems size from a single commercial site to a utility-scale, grid-adjacent installation across our Aura, Aqua, and Ultima product lines without changing the underlying technology.

What global BESS leaders are already proving

India is not pioneering this shift. It is catching up to one. Global battery storage installations crossed 100 GW for the first time in 2025, up 40% year-on-year and eleven times 2021 levels. The diesel-replacement case specifically is already playing out abroad at scale, with large battery installations providing backup and bridging power at data centres in place of diesel generators.

Most of that global capacity, however, is built overseas and imported into India, which is exactly the gap UnityESS is built to close. Its cells and systems come out of a 6-acre manufacturing facility in Ghiloth, Rajasthan, backed by nine years of battery and O&M data across three factories. Proven Before Promised is not a slogan here. It is the difference between importing a system once and buying one from a supplier who commissioned it, monitors it, and will still be answering the phone in year seven.

India’s own numbers are moving fast. India allocated 10.4 GW of standalone BESS capacity in 2025. India’s commercial and industrial (C&I) energy-storage market specifically is projected to grow roughly 30-fold by 2032 as adoption shifts from backup-only deployment to application-driven storage.

Policy signals making BESS the default in Indian industry

The Central Electricity Authority puts India's total storage requirement at 888 GWh by 2035-36, including 321 GWh of battery storage, to meet rising demand and integrate renewable power reliably. The Ministry of Power now mandates battery energy storage systems in new solar and renewable energy projects, making BESS a default component of renewable capacity rather than an optional add-on.

None of this makes diesel disappear overnight, and it shouldn't. A battery sized for two hours of backup cannot ride through a two-day outage, and diesel still has a role in rare, extended events. But for the everyday job diesel generators actually do — daily outages, peak-tariff windows, voltage dips, brownouts — that job now belongs to a system that switches in under 10–20 milliseconds, needs no fuel run, and gets smarter with every cycle.
